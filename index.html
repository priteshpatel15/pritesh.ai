Latest obsession

The agent says it is done. Is it?

Most of what I build now, I build with AI agents. They kept telling me work was finished when it was not. So I stopped reading their reports and started demanding evidence a program can check: a command, its exit code, a trace the agent did not write.

  • sdd-harness. A CLI that will not mark an agent's work complete without command-backed test evidence. 189 tests.
  • agent-output-eval. Scores a deliverable and its trace by counting evidence. No model grades the output.
  • dev-command-library. Keeps a coding agent to small changes, each one checked before the next.
  • A product harness. Compiles a product spec into contracts and review lanes an agent must pass.

This works while the checker is simple and the agent is not trying to fool it. I want to know what holds when neither is true.

The story

Most technical founders are missing a piece.

The engineer cannot sell. The product person cannot build. The data scientist ships a model nobody uses. I spent twenty years closing those gaps, one at a time.

I started as an engineer. At Turner I architected search for CNN, the NBA, TBS and TNT. I learned what software has to survive when millions of people use it.

Then I wanted to know why we were building it. At GE I became a product manager, revived a failing search and machine-learning effort, and was made a director. I pitched a new data product, won $8M to build it, and it returned $50M.

Then I wanted proof. I went back to the keyboard as a principal data scientist. My pricing models added $16M in revenue. My document models became published research. At Walmart I led products that lifted in-stock by $10B and automated $50M of ordering a week.

Then AI changed what a small team can do. At Fisher Phillips I built three AI products myself, hired a team of four, and now run more than 25 agents for 800 lawyers.

Every time, I did the work first and led the team second. I never stopped doing the work.

And I have failed at the part that matters most. I started three restaurant-technology companies and ran pilots with five customers. None found traction. Those three taught me more about customers than any success did.

So I know what to build, how to build it, how to tell whether it is working, how to do it with a handful of people, how to hire those people, and what it sounds like when a customer says no. That is the whole job of starting a technology company. I have done every part of it.

Why four crafts make a whole company

A business needs someone to answer four questions. Most founders can answer one or two and must hire for the rest. I have done each job myself and led a team in it.

  • Product management: what should we build, and for whom? I find the real problem before picking a solution, and I have won funding on that case.
  • Software architecture: will it hold up? I design systems that survive real traffic, from search across Turner's networks to a firm's AI platform.
  • Data science: is it working? I measure outcomes and build the models that move them. I do not ship on instinct.
  • AI engineering: how far can a small team go? I build with agents and know where they fail, so a few people can do the work of many.

Leading a team in all four taught me the fifth skill: hiring and growing the people who do each one. Running my own consulting practice and three startups taught me the sixth: finding customers and hearing no. Put together, I can take a company from the first conversation with a customer to a product in production, and know which parts I am getting wrong.

In between, at Walmart, I led products that lifted e-commerce in-stock by $10B.
